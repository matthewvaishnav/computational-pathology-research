\section{Introduction}

Scanner variation is a persistent problem in computational pathology. Features that distinguish tissue regions may also reveal the scanner that produced them; removing that signal indiscriminately risks removing useful morphological information. A low scanner-classification score therefore cannot, by itself, establish a better tissue representation. The relevant test is whether acquisition-associated information becomes less accessible \emph{while matched regions remain distinguishable}.

Repeated scanning makes that trade-off observable. The same tissue region appears under several scanner acquisitions, providing correspondence supervision without requiring a diagnostic label for each region. SCORPION supplies this paired-scanner setting \cite{ryu2023scorpion}. PA-NF builds on it with two learned feature branches: one optimized for region correspondence, the other explicitly exposed to scanner prediction. Joint reconstruction retains a route for the original feature information, while contrastive pairing and scanner-related objectives constrain what remains accessible through each branch. The method's contribution is the resulting \emph{joint representation objective and controlled evaluation}, not the existence of paired scans or the assumption that its latent factors are uniquely biological.

Three design choices matter. First, multi-positive contrastive learning groups different acquisitions of the same region and contrasts them with other regions. Second, tissue- and acquisition-branch scanner readouts test complementary properties rather than reporting scanner confusion alone. Third, broken-pair controls examine whether an apparent scanner gain survives when true anatomical correspondence is removed. This makes preservation of matched-region identity an explicit condition of a successful result.

The primary test is a registered SCORPION campaign of seven variants, five slide-blocked folds and five seeds (175 fits). Its main control has the same two-branch neural capacity but omits scanner-routing objectives. Cross-backbone experiments and an independent five-scanner canine study extend the comparator analysis. Two distinct ScanComp slides then assess whether the \emph{original frozen} PA-NF checkpoints improve cross-scanner region retrieval outside the training cohort, with registration eligibility determined before representation inference.

We also examine two subsequent decisions faced by pathology systems: how to aggregate patch information into whole-slide predictions, and how to combine institutional contributions when the largest source is not necessarily the most reliable. TransnnMIL is evaluated alongside established multiple-instance learning comparators, including AttentionMIL \cite{ilse2018attention} and TransMIL \cite{shao2021transmil}; PathologyFL tests conditional site weighting against FedAvg under simulated PANDA perturbations and natural CAMELYON17 center shift. These are separate experiments within one research framework, not estimates of a jointly trained, end-to-end causal effect.

Our contributions are (i) a source-authenticated paired-acquisition factorization objective tested against an equal-capacity neural control with joint scanner-recoverability and retrieval criteria; (ii) external frozen-checkpoint retrieval evidence on two physically distinct ScanComp slides, with matching controls and registration constraints; and (iii) separately evaluated whole-slide and institutional aggregation systems that establish their own comparator-specific outcomes. Across these studies, the central methodological principle is to measure \emph{what information survives a nuisance constraint}, rather than optimize nuisance suppression in isolation.
